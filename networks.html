4.1.1 Why Computers Are Connected in Networks

A computer network is two or more computers connected together so they can communicate and share resources. Before networks existed, if you wanted to share a file with a colleague, you would need to copy it onto a floppy disk and physically carry it to their computer. Networks have transformed how we work, learn, and communicate.

Key Benefits of Networking:

📁 File Sharing

Users can access documents, images, and other files stored on any computer in the network without needing to physically transfer them. In a school, this means students can access their work from any computer in the building, and teachers can distribute resources to all students instantly.

🖨️ Sharing Hardware Resources

Expensive hardware like printers, scanners, and storage devices can be shared among many users. Instead of buying a printer for every computer, an organisation might have just a few networked printers that everyone can use. This reduces costs significantly.

🌐 Internet Access

A single internet connection can be shared across all devices on the network through a router. This is far more cost-effective than having separate internet connections for each device.

💬 Communication

Networks enable email, instant messaging, video conferencing, and collaborative tools. Teams can work together on documents in real-time, even if they're in different buildings or countries.

💾 Centralised Data Storage and Backup

Data can be stored on a central server rather than on individual computers. This makes it easier to back up important files, ensures everyone has access to the latest version, and means data isn't lost if a single computer fails.

👤 Centralised User Management

Network administrators can manage user accounts, permissions, and software installations from one central location. When a new employee joins, their account can be set up once and they can log in from any networked computer.

🌍
4.1.2 Types of Networks: LAN and WAN

🏢 LAN - Local Area Network

A LAN covers a small geographical area such as a single building, school, office, or home. All the hardware (cables, switches, routers) is typically owned and maintained by the organisation that uses it.

LANs typically use Ethernet cables or Wi-Fi to connect devices. Because the distances are short, LANs offer high speeds (often 1 Gbps or faster) and low latency.

Examples: School computer network, office network, home network with multiple devices

🌐 WAN - Wide Area Network

A WAN covers a large geographical area, potentially spanning cities, countries, or even the entire world. WANs connect multiple LANs together, allowing communication across vast distances.

WANs often use infrastructure owned by third parties such as telecommunications companies. The connections might use fibre optic cables, telephone lines, or satellite links.

The Internet is the largest WAN - it connects billions of devices across the entire planet.

💡 Key Differences to Remember:
  • Size: LAN = small area (building), WAN = large area (cities/countries)
  • Ownership: LAN = usually owned by one organisation, WAN = often uses third-party infrastructure
  • Speed: LAN = typically faster, WAN = typically slower due to distance
  • Cost: LAN = cheaper to set up and maintain, WAN = more expensive

4.1.3 How the Internet is Structured

The Internet is a global network of interconnected networks. It's not controlled by any single organisation - instead, it's a collaborative system where thousands of different networks agree to connect and share data using common protocols.

🔢 IP Addressing

Every device connected to the Internet needs a unique identifier so that data can be sent to the correct destination. This identifier is called an IP (Internet Protocol) address.

Think of an IP address like a postal address for your computer. Just as the postal service needs your address to deliver a letter, the Internet needs your IP address to deliver data to your device.

IPv4 example: 192.168.1.1 (four numbers 0-255, separated by dots)

IPv6 example: 2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334 (longer format to allow more addresses)

🔀 Routers

Routers are the traffic directors of the Internet. When you send data across the Internet, it doesn't travel in one piece - it's broken into small chunks called packets. Each packet contains the destination IP address.

Routers examine each packet's destination IP address and decide the best path to forward it. They maintain routing tables that contain information about which networks can be reached through which connections.

A single packet might pass through many routers on its journey across the Internet. If one route is congested or unavailable, routers can redirect packets along alternative paths - this makes the Internet resilient and reliable.

4.1.4 Wired vs Wireless Connectivity

Devices can connect to networks either through physical cables (wired) or through radio signals (wireless). Each method has advantages and disadvantages that affect network performance.

🔌 Wired (Ethernet)

Wired connections use Ethernet cables (typically Cat5e or Cat6) to physically connect devices. The electrical signals travel through copper wires, or in high-performance networks, light pulses travel through fibre optic cables.

📡 Wireless (Wi-Fi)

Wireless connections use radio waves to transmit data through the air. Devices connect to a wireless access point (often built into a router) which then connects to the wired network.

Performance Characteristics:

CharacteristicWired (Ethernet)Wireless (Wi-Fi)
Speed Generally faster and more consistent. Modern Ethernet supports 1 Gbps to 10 Gbps. The cable provides a dedicated connection. Generally slower. Wi-Fi 6 can reach around 1 Gbps in ideal conditions, but speeds vary based on signal strength and interference.
Bandwidth Full bandwidth available to each connected device through its dedicated cable. Bandwidth is shared among all devices connected to the same access point. More devices = less bandwidth each.
Latency Very low latency (delay). Signals travel directly through the cable with minimal processing. Ideal for gaming and video calls. Higher latency due to radio transmission, signal processing, and potential interference. Can cause lag in real-time applications.
Range Limited by cable length - typically up to 100 metres for Ethernet before signal degrades. Limited by signal strength - typically 30-50 metres indoors. Walls and obstacles reduce range significantly.
Security More secure - an attacker would need physical access to the cable or network equipment to intercept data. Less secure - radio signals can be intercepted by anyone within range. Requires encryption (WPA2/WPA3) to protect data.
Reliability Very reliable. Not affected by interference from other devices or environmental factors. Can be affected by interference from other Wi-Fi networks, microwaves, Bluetooth devices, and physical obstacles.
Mobility No mobility - devices must stay connected to their cable. Full mobility - users can move freely within range while staying connected.

4.1.5 Network Speeds and Transmission Calculations

Network speeds are measured in bits per second (not bytes). This is because data is transmitted as a stream of individual bits (0s and 1s) across the network. Understanding the units and being able to calculate transmission times is essential.

Speed Units:

1 bps
1 bit per second
1 Kbps
1,000 bits per second
1 Mbps
1,000,000 bits per second
1 Gbps
1,000,000,000 bits per second

📐 The Key Formula

Time (seconds) = File Size (bits) ÷ Transfer Rate (bps)

This can be rearranged to find file size or transfer rate if you know the other values.

⚠️ Critical: Converting Between Bits and Bytes

File sizes are usually given in bytes (KB, MB, GB), but network speeds are in bits per second. Since there are 8 bits in 1 byte, you must convert:

  • To convert bytes to bits: multiply by 8
  • To convert bits to bytes: divide by 8
  • 500 MB = 500 × 8 = 4,000 Mb (megabits)
📝 Worked Example

Question: How long does it take to download a 240 MB file with a 60 Mbps connection?

Step 1: Convert file size to megabits: 240 MB × 8 = 1,920 Mb
Step 2: Apply the formula: Time = 1,920 ÷ 60 = 32 seconds

4.1.6 Network Protocols

A protocol is a set of rules that govern how data is transmitted across a network. Without agreed protocols, devices from different manufacturers wouldn't be able to communicate - it would be like people trying to have a conversation when they speak different languages.

Protocols define things like: how a connection is established, how data is formatted, how errors are detected and corrected, and how the connection is terminated. Different protocols handle different tasks.

Physical/Link Layer Protocols:

Ethernet

The most common protocol for wired local area networks. It defines how data is formatted into frames, how devices are addressed using MAC addresses, and how devices share access to the network cable. When you plug an Ethernet cable into your computer, you're using this protocol.

Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11)

A family of protocols for wireless networking. Wi-Fi defines how devices connect to wireless access points, how data is transmitted over radio waves, and how to handle interference and security. Different versions (Wi-Fi 4, 5, 6) offer increasing speeds and capabilities.

Internet Protocols:

TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol / Internet Protocol)

The fundamental protocol suite of the Internet. IP handles addressing and routing - it ensures packets are sent to the correct destination using IP addresses. TCP handles reliable delivery - it breaks data into packets, numbers them, checks they all arrive, and reassembles them in the correct order.

Together, TCP/IP ensures that when you request a web page, all the data arrives complete and in order, even though it may have taken different routes across the Internet.

Application Layer Protocols:

HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol)

Used to transfer web pages from web servers to browsers. When you type a URL into your browser, it sends an HTTP request to the server, which responds with the HTML, images, and other content that make up the page. HTTP is unencrypted - data is sent in plain text.

HTTPS (HTTP Secure)

The secure version of HTTP. All data is encrypted before transmission, so even if someone intercepts it, they cannot read it. Essential for banking, shopping, and any site handling sensitive information. Look for the padlock icon in your browser's address bar.

FTP (File Transfer Protocol)

Designed specifically for transferring files between computers. It's commonly used by web developers to upload website files to a web server. FTP allows browsing directories, uploading, downloading, and managing files on a remote computer.

Email Protocols:

SMTP (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol)

Used for sending outgoing emails. When you click "Send" in your email client, SMTP handles delivering your message to the recipient's mail server. Think of it as the postal service that carries your letter away.

POP3 (Post Office Protocol version 3)

Used for receiving emails. POP3 downloads emails from the server to your device and typically deletes them from the server. This means your emails are only stored locally on one device - if that device fails, the emails are lost.

IMAP (Internet Message Access Protocol)

A more modern protocol for receiving emails. Unlike POP3, IMAP keeps emails on the server and synchronises them across all your devices. Read an email on your phone, and it's marked as read on your laptop too. This is how most modern email services work.

4.1.7 The TCP/IP 4-Layer Model

The TCP/IP model describes how data is transmitted across the Internet by dividing the process into four layers. Each layer has a specific job and passes data to the layer below it (when sending) or above it (when receiving). This layered approach means that changes to one layer don't affect the others.

Layer 4: Application Layer
HTTP, HTTPS, FTP, SMTP, POP3, IMAP
Layer 3: Transport Layer
TCP, UDP
Layer 2: Internet Layer
IP (Internet Protocol)
Layer 1: Link Layer
Ethernet, Wi-Fi
Layer 4: Application Layer

This is where user applications operate. When you browse the web, your browser uses HTTP/HTTPS at this layer. When you send email, your mail client uses SMTP. The application layer formats the data appropriately for the service being used and passes it down to the transport layer.

Layer 3: Transport Layer

The transport layer is responsible for end-to-end communication. TCP breaks data into smaller segments, adds sequence numbers so they can be reassembled in order, and ensures reliable delivery by requesting retransmission of any lost packets. It also uses port numbers to direct data to the correct application on the receiving device.

Layer 2: Internet Layer

The internet layer handles addressing and routing. It wraps each segment from the transport layer into a packet and adds source and destination IP addresses. Routers operate at this layer - they read the destination IP address and forward the packet towards its destination, potentially across many different networks.

Layer 1: Link Layer (Network Access Layer)

The link layer handles the physical transmission of data over the network hardware. It wraps packets into frames, adds MAC addresses for local delivery, and manages access to the physical network medium (whether that's an Ethernet cable or Wi-Fi). This layer deals with the actual electrical signals or radio waves that carry the data.

📧 Example: Sending an Email

Application: You compose an email and click send. Your email client uses SMTP to format the message.

Transport: TCP breaks your email into segments, adds sequence numbers and port 25 (SMTP's port).

Internet: IP adds your computer's IP address and the mail server's IP address to create packets.

Link: Ethernet/Wi-Fi adds MAC addresses and transmits the frames over the physical network.

At the receiving end, the process reverses - each layer strips off its header and passes the data up until the email appears in the recipient's inbox.

4.1.8 Network Topologies

A network topology describes the arrangement of devices (nodes) and connections (links) in a network. The topology affects performance, reliability, cost, and ease of maintenance. The three main topologies you need to know are Star, Bus, and Mesh.

⭐ Star Topology

In a star topology, every device connects to a central hub or switch. All communication passes through this central device. If Computer A wants to send data to Computer B, the data goes from A to the hub, then from the hub to B.

Advantages:

  • If one cable or device fails, only that device is affected - the rest of the network continues working
  • Easy to add new devices - just connect them to the hub
  • Easy to detect and fix faults - problems are isolated to individual connections
  • Good performance - each device has its own dedicated connection to the hub

Disadvantages:

  • The central hub is a single point of failure - if it fails, the entire network goes down
  • Requires more cabling than bus topology (each device needs its own cable to the hub)
  • The hub can be expensive, especially for large networks

🚌 Bus Topology

In a bus topology, all devices connect to a single main cable (the "bus" or "backbone"). Data sent by any device travels along the entire cable, and all devices receive it, but only the intended recipient processes it. The cable has terminators at both ends to absorb signals and prevent them from bouncing back.

Advantages:

  • Cheap to install - uses less cable than star topology
  • Simple to set up for small networks
  • Easy to extend - new devices just tap into the cable

Disadvantages:

  • If the main cable fails, the entire network fails
  • Difficult to identify faults - problems could be anywhere along the cable
  • Performance decreases as more devices are added (more chance of collisions)
  • Limited cable length restricts network size
  • Security risk - all data passes all devices

🕸️ Mesh Topology

In a mesh topology, every device is connected to every other device (full mesh) or to several other devices (partial mesh). This creates multiple paths for data to travel between any two points. If one connection fails, data can take an alternative route.

Advantages:

  • No single point of failure - the network is highly resilient
  • Data can take the fastest available route
  • Easy to identify faults
  • Can handle high traffic volumes - multiple paths prevent congestion

Disadvantages:

  • Very expensive - requires large amounts of cabling
  • Complex to set up and manage
  • Each device needs multiple network ports
  • Impractical for large networks (the number of connections grows exponentially)

Use case: Mesh topology is commonly used in the backbone of the Internet and for critical systems where reliability is paramount.
